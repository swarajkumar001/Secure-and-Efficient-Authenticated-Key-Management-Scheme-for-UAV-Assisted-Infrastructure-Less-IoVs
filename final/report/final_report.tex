\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Tan, Zheng and Vijayakumar proposed a tethered UAV (TUAV) that takes the place of destroyed roadside units so that an Internet of Vehicles (IoV) keeps
working after a disaster. We studied the scheme equation by equation, implemented it, and found that it does not achieve the property it is named for.
Every authentication is relayed over a satellite link to a cloud Trusted Authority (TA) that performs all pairings, so authentication stops when the
link is lost. The UAV credential depends only on values the TA holds, so the TA can impersonate any UAV; ProVerif finds this attack automatically.
The CRT-based group key leaks every member's decrypting key to every other member, so a revoked UAV still obtains the new key; our implementation
reproduces the attack. The reported cost and traffic omit the TA's pairings and a group-key broadcast that grows as $O(n^3)$ bytes
(17.5\,MB for 120 UAVs under our encoding).

We propose \textbf{Cutting the Cord}, which keeps the TUAV architecture but makes the trust root \emph{offline}. \textbf{C1}: the TUAV
authenticates UAVs itself with escrow-free, pairing-free certificateless signatures in a small-exponent batch with a bounded fallback, and
delivers the group key per member under fresh ECDH keys. \textbf{C2}: vehicles, which the base paper leaves out, are admitted through authenticated
UAVs that relay and filter their requests. \textbf{C3}: neighbouring TUAVs hand members over with single-use encrypted tickets.

ProVerif verifies authentication, key secrecy and forward secrecy for C1 and C3 and revocation for rekeying. It also delimits the escrow guarantee
precisely: a malicious TA cannot sign under a registered key but, as in every certificateless scheme, can enrol a replacement key. On the same curve
and library, our TUAV authenticates and keys $n$ UAVs one to two orders of magnitude faster than the base scheme's TA-inclusive cost. It needs no
satellite traffic, keeps admitting honest UAVs when a batch is poisoned, and reduces key-delivery traffic from megabytes to kilobytes. With the base
paper's own MIRACL constants the analytical figures are 228\,ms against 3720\,ms (plus the satellite round trip) for 120 UAVs.

Colours are consistent: \textcolor{deep}{\textbf{dark blue}} = Trusted Authority, \textcolor{uavc}{\textbf{teal}} = UAV tier (C1),
\textcolor{vehc}{\textbf{violet}} = vehicle tier (C2), \textcolor{hoc}{\textbf{amber}} = TUAV handover (C3), \textcolor{bad}{\textbf{red}} = flaws and attacks,
\textcolor{good}{\textbf{green}} = properties that hold. Chapters~\ref{sec:base}--\ref{sec:findings} use the \emph{base paper's} symbols
($\sigma_i,\Gamma_i,\kappa_{tu},\Lambda(x)$); the rest of the report uses \emph{our} symbols (Table~\ref{tab:notation}). In particular, $\sigma_i$ always means the base
paper's decrypting key, and our signatures are written $(R,v)$.

\paragraph{Three kinds of evidence.} Every claim is marked with how we know it: \emph{analytical} (operation counts times published timings, the method
of the base paper), \emph{measured} (our implementation, Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}), or \emph{formally verified} (ProVerif, Chapter~\ref{ch:pv}).
Proofs in Chapter~\ref{sec:security} are proof sketches that follow standard reductions.
