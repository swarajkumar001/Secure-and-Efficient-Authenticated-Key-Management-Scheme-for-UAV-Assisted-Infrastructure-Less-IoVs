\title{Cutting the Cord: Authority-Independent, Escrow-Free Authentication and Key Management for TUAV-Assisted Infrastructure-Less IoV}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Swaraj Kumar, Hritik Ranjan, Harsh Raj}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Team 6 --- Research in Information Security, October 2026}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A recent scheme by Tan, Zheng and Vijayakumar (IEEE T-ITS 2023) uses a tethered UAV (TUAV) to replace roadside units destroyed by a disaster, so that an
Internet of Vehicles keeps working ``without infrastructure''. We show that it does not achieve this. Every authentication is relayed over a satellite link
to a cloud Trusted Authority (TA) that computes all pairings. The UAV credential depends only on values the TA holds, so the TA can impersonate any UAV.
The CRT-based group key reveals every member's decrypting key to every other member, so a revoked UAV still obtains the new key. The reported costs omit the
TA's pairings and an $O(n^3)$-byte key broadcast. We confirm these findings in code and, for the escrow attack, with ProVerif. We then propose
\emph{Cutting the Cord}, which moves the trust root offline. The TUAV authenticates UAVs itself with escrow-free, pairing-free certificateless signatures in a
sound batch with a bounded fallback. It delivers the group key per member under fresh ECDH keys, admits vehicles through authenticated UAV relays, and hands
members between TUAVs with single-use tickets. Removing the authority creates a problem of its own: nobody online can overrule a forged packet or a captured edge
node. We demonstrate this with \EAttacksVOne\ executed attacks on our own first design, including one forged packet that locks a UAV out for an epoch and a captured
TUAV that reads every neighbouring cell's group key. We fix them and add a containment tier in which a quorum of TUAVs evicts a captured peer with the TA offline. On
a \EBlastCells-cell grid, one captured TUAV then exposes \EBlastHardOne\% of cells instead of \EBlastFirstOne\%. Symbolic ProVerif models of the core protocols verify
authentication, key secrecy, forward secrecy and revocation, including handover with a captured TUAV, and delimit the residual TA power to Girault level~2. On the same curve and library, the base scheme's
TA-inclusive cost is about \RSpeedup$\times$ ours, our scheme needs no satellite traffic, and it sends \RBytesRatio$\times$ less key traffic.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Internet of Vehicles, tethered UAV, certificateless signatures, batch verification, group key management, node capture, revocation, ProVerif.

\section{Introduction}
Roadside units (RSUs) authenticate vehicles in a conventional Internet of Vehicles (IoV). After an earthquake or flood they are among the first things to fail,
exactly when rescue traffic needs secure coordination. Tan \textit{et al.}~\cite{base} propose a \emph{tethered UAV} (TUAV), powered by cable from an
emergency vehicle, as a flying edge server for a swarm of UAVs, with certificateless batch authentication and a CRT-based group key. They report 151\,ms to
authenticate 120 UAVs and eleven satisfied security properties.

We examined the scheme equation by equation, implemented it on BN254, and modelled its credential in ProVerif. The architecture is sound; the protocol is not.
The TUAV cannot verify anyone without a satellite round trip to the TA (so authentication stops when the link fails). The TA can impersonate every UAV. A
revoked UAV keeps receiving group keys. The efficiency figures omit the dominant cost.

Fixing this by taking the authority out of the field is not free. A cloud TA, for all its faults, could overrule a false report and refuse a stolen device. With the TA
offline, a captured TUAV and a forged packet face no such check, and a design that ignores this fails in new ways. Our own first design did.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) Six findings against~\cite{base}, four reproduced in code and one found automatically by ProVerif (Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}).
(2) \emph{Cutting the Cord}: a framework with an offline trust root and four tiers. C1 is autonomous, escrow-free UAV authentication with secure rekeying. C2
admits vehicles through UAV relays. C3 is signature-bound, ticket-based handover between TUAVs. C4 evicts a captured TUAV by quorum (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}).
(3) A compromise analysis of authority-independent admission: \EAttacksVOne\ executed attacks on our first design, their fixes, and the rule behind them: with no
authority online, any state change that outlives a window must rest on evidence a third party can check (Sec.~\ref{sec:contain}). (4) Security arguments and eight
ProVerif models with twenty-three queries, which pin down the residual TA power precisely (Sec.~\ref{sec:security}). (5) An implementation of both schemes on one curve and
library, with nine experiments, including the blast radius of captured TUAVs and the measured cost of containing them (Sec.~\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Background and the Base Scheme}\label{sec:base}
\textbf{Certificateless cryptography}~\cite{alriyami} splits a private key into a partial key issued by the authority and a secret value the authority never
sees. In Girault's terms~\cite{girault}, the authority is then at \emph{level~2}: it cannot learn a user's key, but it could issue a second, detectable key. At
level~1 it knows users' keys. \textbf{Batch verification} needs random small exponents to be sound~\cite{bgr}. \textbf{Group key management} ranges from per-member
(unicast) delivery to key trees (LKH)~\cite{lkh} and single-broadcast CRT ``secure locks''~\cite{securelock}.

\textbf{The base scheme}~\cite{base} has a TA, a TUAV and $n$ UAVs. In IS1--IS5 the TA gives the TUAV $(id_{tu},s_{tu})$ and each UAV $(id_i,s_i)$, and the TUAV
broadcasts $(ts_{tu},ID_{tu},R_{tu},Q_{tu})$. In US1--US3 each UAV picks $r_i$ and sends $(ts_2,ID_i,\xi_i,\upsilon_i,\Gamma_i)$, where $\xi_i=h_1(r_i)$ and
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_i=[s_ih_3(ID_i,ts_2,s_i)R_{tu}-h_1(s_i\xi_i)]P-\upsilon_ih_1(r_i)Q_{tu}.\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
In GA1--GA2 the TUAV uploads the requests to the TA, which computes $\sigma_i=h_1(s_i\xi_i)P$ and three pairings $\psi_i,\mu_i,\delta_i$. In GA3 the TUAV
checks one product equation. In GD1--GD6 the TUAV publishes the coefficients of
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(x)=\kappa_{tu}\textstyle\sum_i\omega_i\varphi_i+\prod_i(x-\sigma_i),\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
with $\omega_i=\prod_{j\ne i}\sigma_j$ and $\varphi_i\equiv\omega_i^{-1}\bmod\sigma_i$. Each UAV recovers $\kappa_{tu}=\Lambda(\sigma_i)\bmod\sigma_i$. In DU1 the
polynomial is rebuilt over the remaining members' \emph{unchanged} $\sigma_i$.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Drone-assisted vehicular security.} Recent schemes pursue lightweight authentication for UAV-assisted vehicular networks~\cite{kumar26,ahmed26} and batch
authentication with tree-based key agreement for UAV swarms~\cite{cubat}. Swarm group authentication has also been built on threshold secret sharing~\cite{aydin}. We
found no published cryptanalysis of~\cite{base}, and no scheme that combines an offline authority with containment of a captured edge node for this setting.
% TODO: confirm the "no published cryptanalysis" claim with a Scholar / IEEE Xplore citation search of [base] before submission.

\textbf{CRT group keys.} Secure locks~\cite{securelock} deliver one key to many recipients through CRT, and Vijayakumar \textit{et al.}~\cite{vijay14} reduced the
server's rekeying cost. Xia \textit{et al.}~\cite{xia23} report that in~\cite{vijay14} a former member may still obtain the group key and colluding members may recover
another member's long-term key. Our finding F4 belongs to the same class of failure. It targets the polynomial construction of~\cite{base} and needs one insider and no
collusion. Newer CRT-based protocols pack per-member ciphertexts into one broadcast integer~\cite{pug}, a different construction that F4 does not address.

\textbf{Revocation without an authority.} Chan \textit{et al.}~\cite{chan05} revoke compromised sensor nodes by neighbours' votes. C4 applies the quorum idea to TUAVs,
using certificateless signatures so that any member can check an eviction with the TA's public key alone.

\section{Analysis of the Base Scheme}\label{sec:findings}
\textbf{F1: not infrastructure-less.} GA3 uses only values computed by the TA in GA2, and the TA is reached by satellite. With link availability $A$, a round
succeeds with probability $A$, and every round pays a satellite round trip.

\textbf{F2: the TA impersonates every UAV.} Since $\xi_i=h_1(r_i)$ is sent in clear, (\ref{eq:gamma}) depends only on $s_i$, which the TA stores, and on public
values. The TA can therefore build a valid request for any UAV (level~1, so claims F4 and F10 of~\cite{base} fail). Verification also needs $s_i$, so no
credential is non-repudiable. Our implementation accepts a TA-built request, and ProVerif finds the attack (Q16, Table~\ref{tab:pv}).

\textbf{F3: no forward secrecy.} $\sigma_i$ is determined by $s_i$ and the public $\xi_i$; a later leak of $s_i$ opens every recorded group key.

\textbf{F4: revocation fails.}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:leak}
For every member $j$: $\Lambda(x)-\Lambda(\sigma_j)=\prod_i(x-\sigma_i)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\Lambda(x)=C+\prod_i(x-\sigma_i)$ with constant $C$, and the product vanishes at $\sigma_j$.
\end{proof}
Any member therefore recovers \emph{all} $\sigma_i$ by integer root finding. Because DU1 reuses them, a revoked UAV computes the new key as
$\Lambda^*(\sigma_j)\bmod\sigma_j$ for any staying $j$. Our code executes this attack. The $\sigma_i$ are also points in GA2 but moduli in GD1, so the
construction is not even well defined as printed.

\textbf{F5: empty proof, unsound batch.} Theorem~1 of~\cite{base} reduces forgery to the CDH instance $(s_{tu}P,(\sum\upsilon_i\xi_i)P)$, whose second exponent is
public, so the instance is trivial. GA3 has no random weights, so $\Gamma_1+\Delta,\Gamma_2-\Delta$ pass while both fail individually (reproduced). GA4 has no
failure branch, so one bad request rejects everyone.

\textbf{F6: cost omits the TA.} GA2 performs three pairings per UAV. With the base paper's own MIRACL constants, group authentication of 120 UAVs costs
3720\,ms plus the satellite RTT, not 151\,ms. The CRT coefficients make each acknowledgement $O(n^2)$ bits, sent to each of $n$ UAVs (Sec.~\ref{sec:eval}).

All six share one root cause: \emph{the TA is inside every operation}.

\section{Cutting the Cord}\label{sec:design}
\textbf{Model.} A TA (offline after deployment); TUAVs $\mathcal T_a$ with public identities; UAVs $\mathcal U_i$ and vehicles $\mathcal V_j$ with pseudonyms.
The attacker is Dolev--Yao on all links, plus Type-I (key-replacing outsider) and Type-II (malicious TA) certificateless adversaries, insiders, and batch poisoners.

\textbf{Setup and registration (offline).} The TA holds $s$ ($\Ppub=sP$) to certify keys and $t$ ($\Tpub=tP$) to trace pseudonyms. For each epoch $\ep$ an
entity picks $x$, sends $X=xP$ with a proof of possession, and receives $\PID=(\rho P,\RID\oplus H_0(\rho\Tpub\cat\ep),\mathit{role})$, $Y=yP$ and
$d=y+sh$, where $h=H_1(\PID,\ep,X,Y)$. The full key is $\sk=x+d$ and $\pk=X+Y+h\Ppub=\sk P$. The TA knows $d$ but never $x$. Epochs start at 1; TUAV credentials use
$\ep=0$, so the two kinds cannot stand in for each other.

\textbf{C1, UAV tier.} The TUAV broadcasts a signed beacon carrying a fresh $E_a=e_aP$. A UAV signs $m_i=(\texttt{join}\cat ID_a\cat E_a\cat E_i)$ with a
Schnorr-type signature $(R_i,v_i)$, $v_i=r_i+c_i\sk_i$ for challenge $c_i$, and sends $\langle\PID_i,\ep,X_i,Y_i,E_i,ts_i,R_i,v_i\rangle$, signing at most once per beacon.
The TUAV filters the requests (freshness, epoch, eviction list, role), keeps at most $c=2$ distinct requests per pseudonym, sampled uniformly from those received, verifies
contested pseudonyms individually, and checks the rest, with random 64-bit $\lambda_i$,
\begin{multline}
\Big(\sum\lambda_iv_i\Big)P=\sum\lambda_iR_i\\
+\sum(\lambda_ic_i)(X_i+Y_i)+\Big(\sum\lambda_ic_ih_i\Big)\Ppub.\label{eq:bv}
\end{multline}
If the check fails, the TUAV verifies each request, admits the valid ones, and stays in individual mode until windows are clean. A failed signature evicts nobody
(Sec.~\ref{sec:contain}). The TUAV then derives $K_i=\KDF(e_aE_i\cat\dots)$ and broadcasts one ACK with $\Enc(K_i,\GK_a\cat\nu)$ for each member. Whenever members join or
leave, at most once per window, rekeying draws a fresh $\GK'_a$ and encrypts it only under the $K_i$ of those who should hold it; members accept only increasing $\nu$.
LKH~\cite{lkh} reduces a single leave to $2\lceil\log_2 n\rceil-1$ ciphertexts.

\textbf{C2, vehicle tier.} Vehicles hold the same kind of credential, and the certified role stops them from claiming the UAV tier. A UAV relays their signed requests in a bundle authenticated by $\MAC(K_i,\cdot)$, so the TUAV
discards bundles from non-members before any signature work. Valid vehicles receive a separate regional key $\VK_a$. Routine V2V beacons carry a MAC under
$\VK_a$; safety-critical events are signed.

\textbf{C3, handover.} Neighbouring TUAVs agree a link key $K_{ab}$ by signed ECDH. Before a member $\mathcal M$ leaves, $\mathcal T_a$
gives it a ticket $\Enc(K_{ab},\PID\cat\pk\cat hk\cat N\cat exp)$, where $hk=\KDF(K_\mathcal M\cat\dots)$. With a fresh $E$, $\mathcal M$ signs
$(ID_a\cat ID_b\cat H(\text{ticket})\cat E)$. $\mathcal T_b$ opens the ticket, rejects reused $N$, verifies a MAC under $hk$ and that signature, runs one fresh ECDH, rekeys
its cell, and sends the new group key with its own signature over $(ID_a\cat ID_b\cat H(\text{ticket})\cat E\cat E_b)$, which $\mathcal M$ checks under $ID_b$.
$\mathcal T_a$ rekeys once $\mathcal M$ has left. $\mathcal T_b$ never learns $K_\mathcal M$. The ticket spares the member the wait for a beacon; it no longer vouches
for either side on its own.

\textbf{C4, containment.} A TUAV that learns of a peer's capture signs $(\texttt{evict-tuav}\cat ID_x)$ under its $\ep=0$ credential. How it learns (tamper sensor, lost
tether, crew report) is outside the protocol. Votes from $t$ distinct other TUAVs form a certificate that anyone checks with $\Ppub$ alone, as a batch. TUAVs holding it
drop $K_{ax}$ and refuse $\mathcal T_x$'s tickets; members holding it refuse $\mathcal T_x$'s beacons.

\textbf{Tracing and revocation.} Offline, the TA recovers $\RID=\PID^2\oplus H_0(t\PID^1\cat\ep)$ and can sign a list of the remaining pseudonyms of a revoked entity.
A TUAV may evict locally on an operator's decision, but neighbours accept an eviction only with a TA-signed list or with evidence: two valid signatures by one
pseudonym under one beacon, which an honest member never produces. Nothing waits for the TA.

\section{Security Analysis}\label{sec:security}
\begin{proposition}
No step of C1--C4 sends to or waits for the TA; verification needs only $\Ppub$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{theorem}[Unforgeability]
Under ECDLP in the ROM, no Type-I or Type-II adversary forges a signature under an honest entity's registered key.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
Type~II: embed the challenge in $X^*$; forking on $H_2$ yields $\sk^*$, hence $x^*=\sk^*-d^*$. Type~I: embed it in $\Ppub$; double forking on $H_2$ and
on $H_1$ yields $(\sk^*-\sk^{*\prime})/(h^*-h^{*\prime})$~\cite{forking}. Unlike~\cite{base}, the challenge is never a public value.
\end{proof}
\begin{theorem}[Batch soundness~\cite{bgr}]
If any request is invalid, (\ref{eq:bv}) holds with probability at most $2^{-63}$ (the weights are odd, leaving 63 free bits).
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Keys]
Under CDH and AEAD security, $K_i$ is known only to $\mathcal T_a$ and $\mathcal U_i$. Session keys survive later compromise of all long-term keys. A removed
member cannot obtain $\GK'_a$, and a joiner cannot obtain earlier keys.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proposition}[Bounded poisoning]
Over $n$ pseudonyms, a window costs at most $T_{BV}(n)+cnT_{SV}$, and a failed signature changes no state beyond the window. A pseudonym that no forged request names is
always admitted. One named by $k$ forged requests is admitted with probability $\min(1,c/(k+1))$ per window.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
Reservoir sampling keeps each of the $k+1$ requests with equal probability, and a kept valid request is verified individually.
\end{proof}
\begin{proposition}[Containment]\label{prop:contain}
Under Theorem~1, a set $\mathcal C$ of captured TUAVs holding no member credential obtains no group key of a cell outside $\mathcal C$. It cannot replace the pairwise key of a
registered member at an honest TUAV, cannot add an honest member to an honest TUAV's eviction list, and, if $|\mathcal C|<t$, cannot evict an honest TUAV.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
Each outcome needs a forgery. Handover admission needs a fresh signature bound to the ticket, the destination and $E$, under a key the TA certified at $\ep\ge1$; the TUAV's
own key is certified at $\ep=0$. Evidence needs two signatures under one beacon, and an honest member signs once. An eviction needs $t$ distinct $\ep=0$ signatures.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Pitfalls of removing the authority}\label{sec:contain}
Reviewing our first design adversarially, we found the attacks in Table~\ref{tab:attacks}. All were executed against its implementation. A1--A4 are design flaws. A5 and A6 are
gaps between its specification and its code, which its tests did not cover. A7 survived the fix for A2 and was found by ProVerif: the member authenticated the new TUAV only
through $hk$, which the captured old TUAV also derives.

A1 deserves attention because the design looked safe. It evicted a pseudonym whose signature failed, as a defence against poisoning. But pseudonyms travel in the clear and stay
valid for a whole epoch, and a failed signature proves nothing about the pseudonym it names. Any outsider who has heard a UAV once could evict it with one packet, and eviction
sharing spread the damage to neighbouring cells. A2 and A3 follow the same pattern. A ticket let $\mathcal T_b$ skip the member's signature, so whoever held $K_{ab}$ could
admit anyone, and neighbours took eviction lists on trust. We had described a captured TUAV as exposing its own cell; through the link keys it exposed every neighbour.

The common rule: \emph{with no authority online, any state change that outlives a window must rest on evidence a third party can check.} A failed signature is not such evidence, and
neither is a neighbour's word.

\section{Implementation and Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
\textbf{Setup.} Both schemes run on BN254 with \texttt{py\_ecc} (pure Python) and \texttt{cryptography}, in one process on one laptop. The base scheme's symmetric
pairing is realised by placing the argument the TA can compute in $\mathbb G_2$. For each $n$ the two schemes run back to back. Because the laptop's speed drifted during the day, we also report a load-independent
ratio from the exact operation counts recorded by the code. Here a pairing costs about \RPairRatio\ scalar
multiplications, against about 8 with MIRACL. BN254 gives roughly 100-bit security today, so a deployment would use a 128-bit curve; we use it for one curve across both
schemes. Fifty-seven tests pass, covering correctness and every attack on both designs. E1--E6 measure the first design. E8 runs both designs side by side and reports the
cost of the fixes, and its absolute times reflect a different machine state from E1--E6, so we compare within each experiment only.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{f2_latency}
\caption{E2: time to authenticate and key $n$ UAVs (log scale). Left: measured. Right: operation counts $\times$ median costs.}
\label{fig:e2}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Latency (E2).} For $n=\RNmax$ our TUAV authenticates and keys the swarm in \ROursTotalS\,s; the base scheme needs \RBaseTotalS\,s before any
satellite delay, and its TUAV-only part (the part~\cite{base} reports) is \RBaseTuavShare\% of that. By operation count the base scheme does
\RCountRatioLo--\RCountRatioHi$\times$ more work at every $n$; measured ratios ($n\le100$) are \RMeasRatioLo--\RMeasRatioHi$\times$. With the base paper's MIRACL constants the
figures are 228\,ms against 3720\,ms plus RTT, about $16\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:e2}).

\textbf{Where the work falls.} Total work is not the drone's work. At $n=\RNmax$ the base scheme's TUAV spends about 3.25\,s (GA3 and GD, from single runs), roughly half of
our \ROursTotalS\,s. We do not lighten the TUAV. We remove the TA's pairings and the satellite trip, and move verification onto a device that the tether powers.

\textbf{Availability (E1).} The base scheme succeeds in a fraction $A$ of rounds; with three retries at $A=50\%$ it reaches \RAvailIidHalfThree\% under independent
losses but only \RAvailBurstHalfThree\% under bursty outages. Ours does not use the link.

\textbf{Poisoning (E3).} One invalid request blocks every honest UAV in the base scheme. Ours admits every UAV whose own request is intact, at every attack rate (E8 covers forged requests that name an honest pseudonym), with the cost bounded
by a failed batch plus individual checks (\RPoisonClean\,s clean, \RPoisonWorst\,s worst). Split-and-retest~\cite{zaverucha} brings no gain because pairing-free
batching is only a few times cheaper per signature (Fig.~\ref{fig:e3}).

\textbf{Traffic (E4).} For $n=120$ the base scheme's acknowledgements carry \RBytesBaseKey\,MB of CRT coefficients and it moves \RBytesBaseSat\,KB over the
satellite link. Our single ACK is \RBytesOursKey\,KB (\RBytesRatio$\times$ less). Our join request is larger (\RReqOurs\,B against \RReqBase\,B).

\textbf{Vehicles and handover (E5, E6).} A bundle of \RVehN\ vehicles is admitted, including keys, in \RVehBatchS\,s, and non-member bundles are dropped in
microseconds. A ticket handover costs the new TUAV \RHoTuavTicket\,ms instead of \RHoTuavFull\,ms. Over a one-hour mobility run with \RHoCross\ cell crossings,
the mean service gap falls from \RHoGapFull\,ms to \RHoGapTicket\,ms. Of that gap, 500\,ms is the assumed mean wait for the next beacon (1\,s period), not a measurement.

\textbf{Attacks (E8a).} All \EAttacksVOne\ attack variants succeed on the first design and \EAttacksVTwo\ of \EAttackTotal\ succeed on the hardened design.

\textbf{Lockout (E8b).} On the first design, one forged request locks a victim out for the rest of the epoch. On the hardened design it costs nothing lasting. With 4 forged requests per
window the victim is admitted in the first window \ELockFourFirst\% of the time (predicted 40\%) and within five windows \ELockFourFive\% of the time; with 32 it still gets in within
five windows \ELockThirtyTwoFive\% of the time (predicted 26.8\%; the standard error is at most 3.5 points over \ELockTrials\ trials). Holding a victim below 50\% over five windows takes about 15 forged requests in every window, instead of one packet in a
15-minute epoch. That is close to jamming, which no protocol prevents. When every pseudonym in a swarm of \ESwarmN\ is spoofed once, the first design admits \ESwarmFirst\ and evicts
\ESwarmEvictedFirst; the hardened design admits \ESwarmHard\ and evicts none, at \ESwarmSpoofS\,s against \ESwarmCleanS\,s for a clean window (\ESwarmCostRatio$\times$), as Proposition~2 bounds.

\textbf{Blast radius (E8c).} On a \EBlastCells-cell grid, one captured TUAV exposes \EBlastFirstOne\% of cells on the first design and \EBlastHardOne\% (its own) on the hardened design; \EBlastCapMax\
captured TUAVs expose \EBlastFirstMax\% and \EBlastHardMax\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:e8}). With \EBlastCapMax\ captured, the first design also lets them displace \EBlastDispFirst\ members and frame \EBlastFrameFirst\ on
average, one attempted per neighbouring cell for each attack; the hardened design allows \EBlastDispHard\ and \EBlastFrameHard.

\textbf{Cost of the fixes (E8d).} Containment is not free, and it removes C3's main computational saving. In a \EHoCell-member cell the new TUAV spends \EHoTuavHard\,ms on a signature-bound
ticket, against \EHoTuavFirst\,ms for a ticket alone and \EHoTuavFull\,ms for full re-admission, because it verifies a signature, does a fresh ECDH and rekeys the cell. The
first design's saving at the new TUAV was the price of trusting the neighbour. The member, which now also checks the new TUAV's signed reply (A7), spends \EHoMemHard\,ms against
\EHoMemFull\,ms, so its saving is gone too. The computation in one handover is \EHoGapHard\,ms against \EHoGapFull\,ms. What remains is latency: full re-admission must first wait
for a beacon, \EHoGapFullWait\,ms with the assumed 1\,s period. These handover figures were re-measured after the fix on a slower machine than the rest of E8; compare them
with each other only. Each crossing also adds \EHoSigBytes\,B for the member's signature, \EHoReplySigBytes\,B for the reply's signature and public key and \EHoRekeyBytes\,B per \EHoCell-member cell rekeyed, and the old cell's rekey takes \EHoDepart\,ms. With half of
\EJoinN\ UAVs arriving, key delivery takes \EJoinHardMs\,ms against \EJoinFirstMs\,ms, and sends \EJoinHardB\,B against \EJoinFirstB\,B. An eviction certificate is \ECertTwoB\,B and \ECertTwoMs\,ms to check at $t=2$, and \ECertFiveB\,B and
\ECertFiveMs\,ms at $t=5$.

\section{Limitations}
A malicious TA keeps Girault level-2 power (detectable key replacement and phantom enrolment). A captured TUAV still exposes its own cell's keys and can run a rogue cell, until it is evicted. C4 assumes that someone detects the capture, that $t$ honest TUAVs can reach each other, and that fewer than $t$ are
captured; $t$ or more captured TUAVs can evict honest ones. We provide no way to restore an evicted TUAV without the TA. An outsider can still flood a window with fresh pseudonyms, and a TUAV needs a
verification budget against this, which we do not evaluate. Members arriving within one window share that window's fresh key. Pseudonyms are linkable within an epoch. Requests are larger. Our timings come from a pure-Python library on a laptop whose speed drifted,
network effects are simulated, and the security arguments are sketches with symbolic models. The ProVerif models cover the signature-bound handover with a captured TUAV but not C4. An embedded constant-time implementation, a
computational proof of containment, and manufacturer-bound keys (level~3) are future work.

\section{Conclusion}
The TUAV architecture of~\cite{base} is a good answer to infrastructure loss, but its protocol keeps the cloud authority inside every step. Moving the trust
root offline restores infrastructure independence, escrow-freeness, forward secrecy and secure revocation together, reduces total work and traffic, and
extends naturally to vehicles and to multi-TUAV handover. It also removes the party that could overrule a forgery or disown a stolen device, and our own first design shows what follows.
Requiring checkable evidence for every lasting state change contains a captured TUAV to its own cell, at the measured cost of the handover's CPU saving; its latency saving remains.
